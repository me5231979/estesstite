## 02 · Why I stopped building decks and started building tools

For most of my career, the deck was the deliverable. A problem showed up. I wrote a proposal, built 30 slides, and walked leadership through the plan. If the meeting went well, I got permission to ask a vendor for a quote. Six months later, maybe, something shipped.

A deck describes a thing that doesn't exist yet. Everyone in the room nods at a different version of it in their head. Then the real thing arrives and half of them are surprised.

A working prototype ends that argument. Nobody debates a tool they can click. They use it, they find what's wrong, and the conversation moves from "should we" to "what's broken."

So I changed the order. I build the first version myself, usually in a few days, and bring that to the meeting instead of the slides.

Take our courses. Custom learning used to take months, and by launch the need had moved. Now my team builds complete interactive course sites with AI coding tools. A 10 to 45 minute course takes 2 to 3 hours of working time, SME intake included. We shipped about 80 of them in 12 months.

The Lab runs on the same pattern. An AI literacy assessment that scores itself and sends a learning path. A simulator where managers practice discipline conversations with state-specific legal stakes. A 9-box calibration tool that runs 36 business units the same way. A course intake pipeline where a request goes in and a built course comes out. Every one of them started as a problem someone said would take a vendor and 6 months.

Speed is the obvious benefit. I think 3 others matter more.

**A tool exposes your thinking.** A deck lets you hide a weak idea behind a good diagram. A prototype breaks in front of you the first time a real user touches it. You find out in a week what a pilot would have told you in a quarter.

**It changes the conversation with executives.** They don't want a learning strategy. They want the problem gone. When I show them something that works, we're talking about their problem in their language.

**It lets a small team cover a lot of ground.** I run a 3-person function serving 4,600 staff. We can't afford work that only lives in a meeting. A tool keeps working after the meeting ends.

There are limits. Some problems are political, and no prototype fixes a leader who doesn't want the change. A tool built fast still needs an owner, standards, and someone to maintain it. That's why I build the first version and then hand it off. Nobody waits on me twice.

I still make decks. They're shorter now, and the last slide is usually a link.

## 03 · AI fluency is a manager problem, not a staff problem

Most organizations are treating AI like a software rollout. Buy the licenses. Send the email. Host a lunch-and-learn. Count how many people logged in.

Then they wonder why nothing changed.

Here's what I see. Staff aren't the bottleneck. Plenty of them already use AI, some quietly, because they aren't sure it's allowed. The bottleneck is the person they report to.

A manager decides whether AI use is encouraged or treated as suspicious. Whether an hour saved gets reinvested or turned into more of the same work. Whether someone who finds a better way gets to share it or gets told to stick to the process. A manager who doesn't understand the tools can't make those calls well, so they default to caution. To the team, caution sounds a lot like "no."

That's why training aimed at staff hits a wall at the first 1:1.

When we built our AI capability work, we started with diagnosis. Everyone was being told to "learn AI," and nobody knew where they stood. We built a self-assessment on a validated research scale. It scores instantly and sends each person a learning path. Then we built a second assessment for managers on the same model, focused on behavior: how they talk about AI with their team, how they handle risk, and whether they make room to experiment.

The second one matters more. It's the reason our broader AI fluency initiative has a dedicated manager track.

Here's what I think a manager track has to cover:

- **Use it yourself first.** You can't coach a tool you've never touched.
- **Redesign the work.** If AI saves your team 5 hours a week, what happens to those hours? That's a management decision, and most managers haven't been asked to make it.
- **Say the guardrails out loud.** People take smart risks when they know where the edges are.
- **Make sharing normal.** The best uses of AI spread sideways, from one person to the next. Managers decide whether that happens.

I learned the underlying lesson building a standard for 677 managers. If you don't define the behavior and measure it, training is a one-time event and nobody can tell whether anything improved. AI fluency works the same way. Decide what a good AI-ready manager does, then check whether your managers are doing it.

Next, I want to map AI exposure by role, so the learning follows the work that's actually changing.

Train your staff. But if you only have budget for one group, start with the managers.

## 04 · The talent marketplace is the new org chart

An org chart tells you who reports to whom. It doesn't tell you who can do what. For a long time that was fine. Jobs were stable, and the box you sat in was a decent stand-in for your skills.

That stopped being true, and the gap shows up in a predictable place. Your best people leave to grow because they can't see a way to grow where they are.

That was the problem we took on. Staff couldn't see where they could go next, so the strongest ones went somewhere else. We didn't answer it with a career development course. We made 2 things visible: what people can do, and what work is open.

We built the staff strategy and communications for a talent platform on Oracle Grow: skills profiles, growth plans, and an opportunity marketplace, all tied to a skills-based job architecture. 90% of staff engaged. In 12 months, Vanderbilt staff made 1,031 internal moves.

Software didn't produce that number. Any organization can buy the platform. What made the difference is how the marketplace changes the way an organization thinks about its people.

The org chart is a map of ownership. A manager "has" a team. A marketplace is a map of capability. People can be found by skill as well as by title, and work can find the people who can do it.

That makes some leaders nervous. If my best analyst can see an opening in another department, I might lose them. True. But I was going to lose them anyway, probably to a competitor. Losing someone to another team in the same organization is the good outcome.

What I'd tell anyone starting:

- **The job architecture is the hard part.** If skills aren't defined consistently, the marketplace is a job board with extra steps.
- **Communications matter more than features.** People have to believe that raising their hand won't be held against them.
- **Managers need a reason to share talent.** If they're measured only on their own team's output, they'll hoard.

Next I'd add short-term gigs, so people can test a new role before they apply for it. That's where the org chart starts to blur, and I think it should.

The org chart isn't going away. Someone still has to approve your PTO. It's just no longer the most useful picture of your organization. The marketplace is.

**Close:** Can your people see where they could grow?

## 05 · Your leadership program should produce projects, not certificates

Ask a leadership program what it produced and you'll usually get 2 numbers: how many people finished and how much they liked it. Both are fine. Neither tells you whether the organization is better led.

Senior leaders don't need more frameworks. Most of them have sat through enough to fill a shelf. What they rarely get is a structured reason to go fix something real, with coaching and cover from the top while they do it.

So when we redesigned our senior program, we made the capstone the point.

Leadership Redefined is sponsored by the Chancellor. It runs 6 modules, 3 coaching sessions, and a weekly synthesis. Every cohort has to ship a real project and pitch it to leadership at the end. The modules are there to serve the project.

116 leaders have completed it across 3 cohorts. 94% were satisfied, which is nice. I care more about 2 other numbers: 98 of those leaders are still at the university, and capstone projects are running in operations today. Those projects kept working after the program ended. A certificate can't do that.

Here's why I think projects beat certificates.

**They force a decision.** A case study lets you discuss what someone else should have done. A capstone makes you decide what you're going to do, with your name on it.

**They give the CFO something to count.** "Leaders feel more confident" is a hard sell in a budget meeting. A list of projects now running in operations is an easy one.

**They build the network that matters.** Leaders working a real problem have to get help from people outside their unit. Those relationships outlast the cohort.

**They show you who's ready.** You learn more about someone's leadership by watching them push a project through resistance than you will from a 360.

Here's what I haven't finished. Not every capstone had a clear business owner. A few good ideas stalled after the pitch because nobody outside the cohort was accountable for them. From here on, every capstone gets a named business owner and a 6-month outcome check.

A cohort only reaches a few dozen people at a time, so we also built a half-day symposium format to bring the same ideas to hundreds of managers.

If your leadership program ends with a ceremony and a PDF, ask what's different 6 months later. If you can't name it, the program is producing certificates.
