Managing people is the real job.

Welcome to Manager Essentials.

Manager Essentials is Vanderbilt’s self-paced foundation for people managers: the compliance you carry, the conversations you will have, and the operations you run. It is built around practice, not slides, so every rule comes with a working simulation where your choices have consequences and the feedback is immediate.

By the end of your engagement, you will:

  • Know the compliance baseline you carry, and what your team’s state adds to it.
  • Practice the hard conversations before they are real, with coaching on every choice.
  • Run an FMLA request and a discipline case end to end without a misstep.

~30 minutes · self-paced · nothing tracked

Start with the rules

How it works

One path. Three stops. Your pace.

Know the rules first, so nothing later takes you by surprise. Then master the conversations, because most management problems are handled or worsened in a single meeting. Then run the operations, where the routine decisions live. Each stop pairs short reading with a working simulation.

The rules you carry.

In this section, you will review the compliance baseline every Vanderbilt manager carries. Eight topics, one state check, and six judgment calls. None of it requires memorizing policy text; it requires knowing what each topic asks of you, and who to call when it shows up.

The idea

Compliance is not a manager’s specialty; it is a manager’s floor. You are not expected to investigate, diagnose, or decide legal questions. You are expected to do three things well: recognize the situations these topics describe, report or consult through the right channel, and never punish anyone for raising a concern. The experts handle the rest.

One more thing before the cards: the moment you became a manager, your words changed weight. A casual remark from a peer is a remark. The same remark from a supervisor can become evidence of how the university treats people. That is why this baseline exists.

The evidence

Why the eighth card exists. Federal enforcement guidance treats supervisors differently from everyone else: because a supervisor speaks with the employer’s authority, the employer carries heightened responsibility for what supervisors do, say, and tolerate. That is the legal shape behind a simple habit: as a manager, you act on what you see and hear, even when nobody has filed anything.

Module C

Whose call is it?

In this activity, you will read six quick situations and decide: report now, consult first, or your call. Answer each one, then read the reasoning. The point is not a perfect score; it is calibrating your instincts before a real one arrives.

You’ll learnWhich situations leave you no discretion You’ll doMake six judgment calls Time3 min

Why it matters to your work: managers rarely get in trouble for what they escalated. They get in trouble for what they sat on.

The idea

Three buckets cover almost everything. Report now: situations where policy or law removes your discretion, such as anything touching the safety of a minor. Consult first: situations where you keep a role but should not act alone, such as medical information or a possible conflict of interest. Your call: ordinary management inside your unit’s norms. When in doubt, move one bucket to the left.

Situation 1 of 6

A parent volunteer in your summer program tells you a staff member was “way too rough” with a camper yesterday.

Situation 2 of 6

A team member mentions, in passing, that a coworker keeps making comments about her accent and “where she is really from.”

Situation 3 of 6

An employee asks to shift their start time 30 minutes earlier for the semester so they can make school pickup.

Situation 4 of 6

A vendor whose contract you manage offers you two tickets to a bowl game as “a thank-you for a great year.”

Situation 5 of 6

A local reporter emails asking for your department’s position on a contested ballot measure.

Situation 6 of 6

An employee tells you, in confidence, that a medical condition has been making their workload hard to manage.

You are here: the conversations. This is where management is actually practiced. Employee Relations / 02

What it means to be a manager.

In this section, you will practice the part of the job nobody can do for you: the conversations. A three-turn difficult conversation with coaching on every choice, an eight-situation sorting drill, and a progressive discipline case you run from first miss to final decision.

The idea

Three habits separate managers who handle employee relations well from managers who create cases. First, your words carry the university’s weight: in a dispute, what you said becomes what Vanderbilt said. Second, document facts, not conclusions: “missed the March 3 and March 12 deadlines” is a record; “has a bad attitude” is an opinion that will be read back to you. Third, never surprise HR: Employee Relations can defend a process it helped build, and almost nothing it first hears about after the fact.

Module A

The difficult conversation

In this activity, you will run one conversation about chronic missed deadlines, three turns long. At each turn, pick what you would actually say. Every choice gets coaching, and the strongest choices follow one model: Situation, Behavior, Impact.

You’ll learnSBI: facts first, no diagnosis, listen before solving You’ll doChoose your reply, three times Time4 min

Why it matters to your work: the first two minutes of a hard conversation usually decide whether it becomes a turnaround or a grievance.

The setup

Jordan, a senior coordinator on your team and a strong performer for three years, has missed three report deadlines in the past month: March 3, March 12, and March 20. Two colleagues had to cover. You have booked 30 minutes with Jordan this afternoon.

A guided practice with scripted coaching, not a live conversation partner. Real cases vary; when one lands, your Employee Relations partner comes before your calendar invite.

The evidence

SBI is not folklore. The Situation-Behavior-Impact model was developed by the Center for Creative Leadership as a way to deliver feedback people can actually use: anchor the moment, describe observable behavior, and name its effect, without labels or diagnosis. It is taught to managers worldwide for the same reason it anchors this module: it keeps hard conversations factual, specific, and fair.

Source: Center for Creative Leadership, the SBI feedback model, ccl.org.

The hard rules

Some categories remove your discretion. Anything touching a protected class, possible harassment, a threat, medical information, or a minor routes to consult or escalate, every time, no matter how small it seems or how much the person asks you to keep it between you. Everything else is judgment, and this drill builds it.

You are here: the operations. The unglamorous approvals where a manager’s consistency shows. Operations / 03

The work that runs on rhythm.

In this section, you will review the baseline responsibilities you sign off on: hiring, pay and time off, and expenses. Then you will walk the operational moment managers most often mishandle, an FMLA request, from the first mention to the return-to-work conversation.

The idea

Every approval you sign is a certification, not a courtesy. A timesheet approval says the hours happened. An expense approval says the purchase was legitimate. A posting says the requirements are real. The habit that keeps all three honest is the same one from the last section: consistency. Approve by the standard, not by the person, and most operational risk never gets the chance to exist.

Module A

Your baseline responsibilities

In this activity, you will open three cards and review what each approval actually asks of you. Every approval you sign is a statement that you checked. These cards are the checklist.

You’ll learnWhat hiring, time, and expense approvals ask of you You’ll doOpen three cards Time2 min

Why it matters to your work: operational approvals are where fairness becomes visible. Teams forgive a hard decision; they do not forgive inconsistent ones.

Hiring

Your role in postings, interviews, and references.
Your job as a manager
  • Postings: write the role around real requirements, and route every posting through the official process rather than informal channels.
  • Interviews: ask every candidate the same core questions, keep them job-related, and never ask about family plans, health, age, or anything near a protected class.
  • References and offers: reference checks and offer terms run through HR, not through side conversations. No verbal promises about pay or title.

Process links: posting, interviewing, and reference-check guides

Pay & PTO

Approving time honestly and knowing when overtime rules apply.
Your job as a manager
  • Timesheets: approve what actually happened, on time. An approval is a certification, not a formality.
  • Overtime awareness: non-exempt employees are paid for all hours worked, including the “quick evening email.” If you allow the work, you owe the time.
  • PTO: apply the same approval standards to everyone, plan coverage early, and never treat requesting leave as a performance problem.

Process links: timekeeping and leave policies

Expense reports

Approver duties and the red flags worth a second look.
Your job as a manager
  • Approve substance, not speed: business purpose, receipts, and the right account. If you cannot explain the expense, do not approve it.
  • Red flags: round numbers, split transactions just under a threshold, weekend charges without a work reason, and repeated missing receipts.
  • When in doubt: ask the question before approving. A five-minute clarification beats a finding later.

Process links: expense and travel policy, approver guide

Your operational calendar: time approvals on a payroll rhythm, expense approvals as they arrive, annual reviews and training deadlines on a yearly cycle. Put each on your actual calendar once, and this entire module becomes routine.

The evidence

The overtime line is federal law, not local custom. The Fair Labor Standards Act requires that non-exempt employees be paid for all hours worked, including work a manager merely allows, such as the evening email answered from the couch. The federal baseline is time and a half past 40 hours in a week; some states add more. If you permit the work, the time counts.

Prove it to yourself.

In this section, you will answer eight questions covering compliance, employee relations, and operations. Nobody sees your score, and you can retake it anytime. When you finish, tell us how we did: two ratings and an optional note.

You’ll learnWhere your gaps are (if any) You’ll doAnswer eight questions, self-scored instantly Time3 min

Why it matters to your work: a short self-test right after learning locks in more than an hour of rereading.

The idea

A good score means the core moves stuck: recognize, document facts, route through the right channel, never surprise HR. A missed question is not a failure; it is a pointer back to the module worth a second look. And no quiz can measure the habit that matters most, which is picking up the phone to your ER partner one conversation earlier than feels necessary.

1/8An employee mentions upcoming surgery and asks about time off. Your first move?

Connect, do not decide. Eligibility is determined by HR / absence management. Your role is recognition, a prompt connection, and no probing for medical details.

2/8Which line belongs in your notes about a performance issue?

Facts with dates. Documentation records behavior, not conclusions or diagnoses. Facts hold up; opinions get read back to you.

3/8An employee reports a coworker’s repeated comments about her religion and asks you to keep it between you. You:

Report it. Possible harassment of a protected class removes your discretion. Be honest with the employee about your reporting duty, and never promise confidentiality you cannot keep.

4/8Under the federal FMLA baseline, eligible employees may take up to:

12 weeks, job-protected, unpaid at the federal baseline, with health coverage continued. Manager approval is not part of eligibility; Vanderbilt’s administration adds its own process on top.

5/8Coaching has not worked and you are ready for a formal written warning. The modeled next step is:

ER before formal steps. Never surprise HR. Employee Relations checks sequence and consistency before anything formal is issued.

6/8Something suggests a minor in your program may be unsafe. You:

Immediately, always. Suspected minor-safety issues are the one category with zero manager discretion and zero waiting.

7/8Your Nashville-based team includes a remote employee in California. Which is true?

Baseline plus the employee’s state. Rules follow where the employee works. California layers additional requirements on top of the baseline; confirm the specifics with HR compliance.

8/8While an employee is on FMLA leave, it is appropriate to:

Protect the leave. No work-contact pressure, no retaliation, and an equivalent role on return. Coverage is your problem to solve, not theirs.

What you now know.

In this section, you will revisit the six ideas you picked up today. Each one is short enough to carry into Monday.

The baseline you carry

Eight compliance topics travel with every Vanderbilt manager, and your team’s state adds a layer on top. Your role is recognition and routing, never investigation.

At work: when a situation matches a topic, your first question is “whose call is this?”

Facts, not conclusions

“Missed the March 3 and March 12 deadlines” is a record. “Bad attitude” is an opinion that will be read back to you. Dates and behavior, every time.

At work: a two-line dated note after a conversation is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.

Never surprise HR

Employee Relations can defend a process it helped build. Consult before formal steps, before promises, and one conversation earlier than feels necessary.

At work: the call to ER is a strength signal, not an admission of failure.

Situation, Behavior, Impact

Hard conversations start with facts and dates, name the behavior rather than the person, and describe the impact. Then you stop talking and listen.

At work: SBI turns “you are unreliable” into a conversation someone can actually act on.

The FMLA trigger

You do not decide eligibility, and you never ask for a diagnosis. Recognize the mention, connect the person with HR / absence management promptly, and protect the leave.

At work: the safe sentence is “thank you for telling me; let me connect you with the right people today.”

Consistency is the shield

Coach early, document facts, set expectations in writing, involve ER before formal steps, and follow the same sequence for everyone. Skipped steps are how cases are lost.

At work: the question “would I do this for anyone on the team?” catches most mistakes before they happen.
