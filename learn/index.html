Module C

Myths vs. facts

Six claims you'll hear in the break room. Tap each one for the verdict.

You’ll learnWhich common AI claims are true You’ll doTap six claims for verdicts Time2 min

Why it matters to your work: you’ll be the calm, informed voice in the room the next time AI comes up at work.

Myth

It predicts patterns in text. There's no understanding, belief, or intent behind the words — which is exactly why your judgment still matters.

Fact

They're called hallucinations: fluent, plausible, and wrong. Fake citations, invented policies, wrong dates. Verify anything that matters.

Mostly myth

AI takes over tasks, not whole jobs. Staff who learn to delegate the repetitive parts get hours back for the work only a person can do.

Myth

Consumer tools may store or learn from what you paste. Anything sensitive or institutional belongs in Amplify, Vanderbilt's approved platform.

Fact

Drafting, summarizing, and reformatting are where it shines. You bring the facts and the judgment; it brings the typing speed.

Myth

If you can write a clear email to a new colleague, you can write a great prompt. The Playground below will prove it.

Ethics / 05

Where policy ends, judgment begins.

Ethics is what you reach for when the handbook hasn't caught up yet. No compliance checkboxes here — five lenses for thinking clearly, and four dilemmas to practice on.

You’ll learnFive lenses for AI judgment calls You’ll doWork through four workplace dilemmas Time4 min

Why it matters to your work: sooner or later one of these lands on your desk — it’s far easier to think it through now than at 4:55 p.m. on a Friday.

Bias & fairness

Models learn from oceans of human writing and decisions — and inherit human bias along the way. In hiring or admissions support, “the algorithm said so” can quietly repeat old unfairness at new speed.

Transparency & disclosure

People deserve to know when AI meaningfully shaped something they’re reading, judging, or relying on. Disclosure isn’t a confession — it’s respect.

Accountability

You own what you send. “The AI did it” has never once worked as a defense, and it never will. If your name is on it, your judgment is in it.

Academic & professional integrity

Universities run on attribution, originality, and trust. Passing off AI work as purely your own spends credibility the whole institution depends on.

Human dignity & labor

These tools were trained on human work, and automation changes colleagues’ days. Involve people in changes that affect them — don’t let efficiency decisions simply happen to them.

Practice

What would you do?

Pick your honest instinct, then compare notes. Reasonable people disagree on these — the goal is knowing why you'd choose, not passing a test.

Scenario 01

The confident citation

A colleague asks you to look over a report before it goes to their director. It reads beautifully — clearly AI-assisted — but one citation is to a study you’re fairly sure doesn’t exist.

Our take: the report can’t go out with a fabricated source — that part isn’t negotiable. But start with the colleague: this is almost always ignorance, not fraud. Escalate only if it doesn’t get fixed.

Principle: Accountability & integrity
Scenario 02

The accommodation email

You’re drowning in email. A long message arrives from a student describing their disability accommodations. You’re tempted to paste it into a public AI tool for a quick summary.

Our take: this is FERPA territory, possibly HIPAA-adjacent. If AI touches it at all, only in Amplify — and for a short, personal, high-stakes message, your own attention might be the whole point.

Principle: Human dignity & data protection
Scenario 03

The name test

Your unit pilots an AI tool that pre-screens job applications. You run a quiet test: two nearly identical resumes, different names — and they come back with noticeably different scores.

Our take: models inherit bias from the data they learned on. Document it, report it, and insist a human owns every hiring decision. This is exactly where “the AI ranked them” fails everyone.

Principle: Bias & fairness
Scenario 04

The grant question

AI helped you substantially draft a grant proposal — structure, phrasing, a first pass at the narrative. The ideas and data are yours. The sponsor’s forms don’t ask about AI use. Say anything?

Our take: where the rules are unsettled, default to the disclosure you’d be comfortable defending in two years. University trust is slow to earn, quick to lose — and norms are moving toward disclosure, not away from it.

Principle: Transparency & integrity

Responsible AI / 06

Use it boldly. Use it well.

Six habits that keep you — and Vanderbilt — on solid ground. They take seconds and save careers. (For the deeper judgment calls behind these habits, see Ethics, just above.)

You’ll learnSix guardrail habits and the never-paste list You’ll doRead it once, remember it forever Time2 min

Why it matters to your work: these habits take seconds and protect students, colleagues, and your own good name.

Verify outputs

AI writes confidently even when it's wrong. Check names, numbers, dates, and citations before anything leaves your desk.

Protect data

FERPA and HIPAA don't pause for convenience. Student records, health info, and anything confidential go to Amplify only — or nowhere.

Disclose AI use

If AI helped draft something substantive, say so. Simple transparency ("drafted with AI assistance, reviewed by me") builds trust.

Human makes the call

AI advises; you decide. Hiring, grading, funding, discipline — decisions about people always need a person accountable for them.

Bias awareness

Models learn from human writing, biases included. Watch for skewed assumptions, especially in anything touching admissions, hiring, or evaluation.

Keep learning

These tools change monthly. Fifteen curious minutes a week keeps your skills compounding while everyone else is still skeptical.

Never paste these into a public AI tool

  • Social Security numbers
  • Student records or grades (FERPA)
  • Health information (HIPAA)
  • Salaries & personnel files
  • Donor records & gift data
  • Passwords, keys & credentials
  • Unpublished research data
  • Contracts & legal matters

Wellbeing / 07

AI saves time — if you let it.

The promise was that AI reduces work. The research says: only if you're deliberate. A February 2026 Harvard Business Review study — “AI Doesn’t Reduce Work—It Intensifies It” by Ranganathan & Ye, who followed about 200 employees at a U.S. tech company for eight months — found AI often intensifies work instead. Three patterns to watch, and a counter-move for each.

You’ll learnHow AI quietly intensifies work — per the HBR research You’ll doSpot the three patterns, run a six-question self-check Time3 min

Why it matters to your work: the hour AI saves you only counts if you actually get to keep it.

Task expansion

When AI makes tasks feel lighter, people don’t do less — they take on more. In the study, workloads grew because “just one more thing” started feeling free.

On campus: you automate the weekly report Monday… and volunteer for two new committees by Thursday.

Counter-move: bank the savings. Decide in advance where saved time goes — and don’t reinvest all of it in new tasks.

Boundary blur

AI makes micro-work so easy it seeps into moments that used to be recovery — breaks, commutes, evenings. The workday stops having edges.

On campus: a “quick” prompt session at 9:40 p.m., because it “only takes a minute.”

Counter-move: AI hours ≠ more hours. If it happens in a break, it’s work. Protect recovery time like you’d protect a meeting.

Multitasking load

Running several AI threads at once feels productive, but the constant cognitive switching adds real fatigue — and the study links it to worse output.

On campus: three chat tabs, a draft memo, and a Copilot pane — and somehow nothing actually finished.

Counter-move: sequence, don’t parallelize. One AI-assisted task at a time: finished, then the next.

Self-check

Intensity self-check

Six honest yes/nos. Nothing is stored or sent anywhere — this one is strictly between you and you.

Q1I fill former micro-breaks (coffee, corridors, waiting rooms) with “quick” AI tasks.
Q2I often run several AI threads or drafts at the same time.
Q3My to-do list has grown since I started using AI.
Q4AI-drafted work follows me into evenings or weekends.
Q5I feel behind if I’m not using AI for everything.
Q6Time AI saves gets refilled with more tasks by default.

Answer all six and I’ll tally you up — honestly is the only rule.

Build your AI practice

  • Intentional pauses: decide together, as a team, when not to use AI.
  • Sequence work: one AI-assisted task at a time, finished before the next begins.
  • Keep human grounding: real conversations and unhurried judgment time stay on the calendar.
  • Personal rule: AI saves an hour → the hour is yours. Spend some of it on nothing at all.

If work feels heavier, say so

Workload creep is an organizational pattern, not a personal failing. Talk to your manager about what AI is quietly adding to your plate — renegotiating scope is a professional move, not a complaint.

Knowledge check / 08

Prove it to yourself.

Eight quick questions — a self-assessment, not an exam. It's unscored and untracked, nothing leaves this page, and you can retake it as many times as you like.

You’ll learnWhere your gaps are (if any) You’ll doAnswer eight questions, self-scored instantly Time3 min

Why it matters to your work: a two-minute self-test locks knowledge in better than an hour of rereading.

1/8A colleague wants AI help summarizing notes that include student grades. Which tool?

Amplify. Student records are FERPA-protected — they only belong in Vanderbilt's approved platform.

2/8Under the hood, what is a large language model actually doing?

Predicting the next word. That's the whole trick — which is why it's brilliant at drafting and unreliable on facts.

3/8An AI gives you a beautifully written answer with two citations. What now?

Verify it yourself. Models fabricate plausible-looking citations. Asking "are you sure?" just gets you a confident yes.

4/8What's a “hallucination” in AI-speak?

Made-up but confident. The model predicts what sounds right, not what is right — so wrong answers arrive sounding great.

5/8Which prompt will get the best result?

Role + task + context + format. The five ingredients from the Prompt Lab. Specific in, useful out.

6/8You're buried in Excel and need formula help. The natural first stop?

Copilot. Office apps → Copilot is the rule of thumb. It can see your sheet, so you skip the copy-paste dance.

7/8Before pasting anything into an AI tool, the one question to ask is:

Sensitive data check. One second of "should this leave Vanderbilt?" prevents the mistakes that actually matter.

8/8You crank the temperature setting way up. The model's output gets…

More random. High temperature gives unlikely words a bigger chance — great for brainstorming, bad for budget memos.
