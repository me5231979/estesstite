    Module A

    The five terms

    In this activity, you will flip five cards: a plain definition and a Vanderbilt example for each term. (Click or press Enter.)

    You’ll learnWhat AI, ML, GenAI, LLMs, and agents really are You’ll doFlip five cards Time3 min
    The idea

    These five terms nest inside each other like dolls. AI is the umbrella: any software doing things that used to need human judgment. Machine learning is how modern AI gets built: instead of a programmer writing rules, the system finds patterns in thousands of examples. Generative AI is machine learning that produces new things (text, images, code) instead of just sorting or scoring. And a large language model is the specific generative engine inside ChatGPT, Amplify, and Copilot. The newest doll is agentic AI: an LLM handed tools and a loop so it can carry out multi-step work on its own. It runs on the same engine as the chatbots, so it inherits the same limits.

    Notice as you flip: the common thread is that none of these systems understand anything. They recognize patterns, and we’ll keep coming back to that one fact.

    The idea

    The model learned one thing from a huge slice of human writing: which word tends to come next. It writes by predicting that word, over and over. It never checks anything; there is no lookup step, only pattern completion (unless the tool explicitly searches the web).

    “The staff meeting has been moved to ?”

    1.0

    Prediction: it scores every word it knows and usually picks a high scorer: no plan, just statistics.

    Temperature: slide it up and unlikely words get a real chance: great for brainstorming, risky for facts.

    Hallucinations: it predicts what sounds right rather than checks what is right, so always verify.

    Myths vs. facts

    In this activity, you will tap six claims you will hear around campus and see the verdict on each.

    You’ll learnWhich common AI claims are true You’ll doTap six claims for verdicts Time2 min
    Myth

    It predicts patterns in text. There's no understanding, belief, or intent behind the words, which is why your judgment still matters.

    Fact

    They're called hallucinations: fluent, plausible, and wrong. Fake citations, invented policies, wrong dates. Verify anything that matters.

    Mostly myth

    AI takes over tasks, not whole jobs. Staff who learn to delegate the repetitive parts get hours back for the work only a person can do.

    Myth

    Consumer tools may store or learn from what you paste. Anything sensitive or institutional belongs in Amplify, Vanderbilt's approved platform.

    Fact

    Drafting, summarizing, and reformatting are where it shines. You bring the facts and the judgment; it brings the typing speed.

    Module E

    Five prompting techniques

    In this module, you will learn five prompting techniques, each with an example you can copy. Paste one into your tool, swap in your details, and send it.

    The idea

    The model completes your pattern: a vague prompt gets confident generic filler back. Each technique narrows the pattern until the likeliest continuation is the one you wanted.

    1

    Show examples (few-shot)

    The fastest way to get your style is to show it, not describe it.

    Here are two subject lines I like: “Quick favor, big impact” and “Three things before Friday.” Write five more in the same voice.
    2

    Iterate: the first answer is a first draft

    Treat it like one. The second ask is usually the one that lands.

    Good start. Now make it half as long, twice as warm, and end with one clear next step.
    3

    Give it your document

    Grounding the model in your text beats its memory every time.

    Using ONLY the policy text below, answer: can a new hire carry over vacation days? Quote the relevant line. [paste policy]
    4

    Ask it to critique you, or itself

    It is a sharp editor, especially of its own work.

    Before you finalize, list three ways this draft could fail with a busy reader. Then revise it to fix all three.
    5

    Start fresh when it drifts

    Long chats accumulate confusion. A clean start beats wrestling with it.

    New chat: You are an experienced editor. Context you need: [two-sentence summary of where we left off]. Task: continue from there.

    You are here: your own job, before any technique. AI readiness / 02

    Your role, task by task.

    In this section, you will look at a role the way AI actually meets it: task by task.

    The idea

    AI does not show up for a job title; it shows up for a task. Some of your tasks sit in AI’s wheelhouse and others barely register, so the task list is the right unit to analyze. One rule underneath everything here: exposure is a planning signal for development, never by itself grounds for eliminating a role.

    Module A

    How AI meets your work

    In this activity, you will read four short ideas: the lens the assessment below applies to your task list.

    You’ll learnExposure, the jagged frontier, and skills churn You’ll doRead four ideas and the evidence under each Time3 min

    01AI exposes tasks, not jobs

    AI meets a role unevenly: the weekly report is highly exposed, the vendor negotiation barely at all. Ask “which of my tasks does AI touch?” and you get a plan instead of a worry.

    The evidence

    About 80 percent of the US workforce could see at least 10 percent of their tasks affected by large language models, and about 19 percent could see half or more affected. The team rated occupations task by task: exposure lives in the task list, not the job title.

    Source: Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin, and Rock (2023), “GPTs are GPTs,” later published in Science, arxiv.org/abs/2303.10130.

    02Exposure is not elimination

    An exposed task is a task AI can help with, not a task that disappears. The practical question for each one: automate it with oversight, or redesign it as human plus AI?

    The evidence

    Real usage splits about 57 percent augmentation to 43 percent automation. Most exposed work is being reshaped with a person in the loop, not removed.

    Source: Anthropic Economic Index (2025), anthropic.com/news/the-anthropic-economic-index.

    03The frontier is jagged

    AI is strong on some tasks and quietly bad at adjacent ones. The only way to find the line for your work is to test task by task, which is why verification matters as much as prompting.

    The evidence

    Inside the frontier, consultants with AI finished 12.2 percent more tasks, worked 25.1 percent faster, and produced over 40 percent higher quality. On a task outside the frontier, they were 19 percentage points more likely to get it wrong. A field experiment with 758 BCG consultants: same people, same tool, opposite results, depending on the task.

    Source: Dell’Acqua, McFowland, Mollick, Lifshitz-Assaf, Kellogg, Rajendran, Krayer, Candelon, and Lakhani (2023), Harvard Business School working paper 24-013, papers.ssrn.com. Ethan Mollick, whose work grounds this platform’s research base, is a co-author.

    04Skills churn is the planning horizon

    When core skills change fast, the response runs in a fixed order: upskill first, reskill where tasks shift, redesign roles where the shift is large, and only then hire.

    The evidence

    Employers expect 39 percent of workers’ core skills to change by 2030. Skills churn, not headcount, is the planning horizon.

    Source: World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs Report 2025, weforum.org.
    The four moves
    1. Inventory the tasks. Write down what your week actually contains; the list, not the job description, is the raw material.
    2. Sort into three buckets. Delegate with oversight, redesign together, or human-core. Every task goes in exactly one.
    3. Name the skills. Each bucket demands something: review habits for delegated work, fluency for redesigned work, depth for the human core.
    4. Turn it into action. A development plan for yourself, or per person if you manage the role; an inventory that never becomes a conversation changes nothing.

    Development plans are yours to run. Role redesign, reclassification, and staffing changes run through HR.

    You are here: how to talk to it. CRIT / 03

    The CRIT prompt framework.

    In this section, you will learn CRIT, the four-part way to talk to AI: Context, Role, Interview, Task. By the end, you will have built and copied a CRIT prompt of your own.

    You’ll learnThe four parts of CRIT, and why the Interview changes the result You’ll doWatch the creator walk it through, then build one in the builder Time4 min + video (optional)
    The idea

    Prompting is not magic: a prompt is instructions handed to a word-probability engine, and no four-letter formula is a spell. What a framework buys you is discipline; what sets CRIT apart is the Interview, which flips the roles so the model asks and you stay the thought leader. No single formula is magic; structure and iteration are what matter.

    C · Context
    What is going on

    The background, the situation, and the constraints, so the model is not guessing in the dark.

    R · Role
    Whose perspective

    The professional lens you want the answer from: an editor, an analyst, a challenger.

    I · Interview
    The signature move

    Tell it to ask you clarifying questions first and wait, so it surfaces its blind spots instead of papering over them.

    T · Task
    The deliverable

    What type of output, what scope, what format: the model can only hit a target you name.

    The source

    CRIT comes from Geoff Woods, best-selling author of The AI-Driven Leader. The Interview is your verification habit moved upstream: gaps surface before drafting instead of during checking.

    Geoff Woods, “Prompting Isn’t Magic, And Why CRIT Is My Preferred Prompt Framework” (AI for Insights Leaders) · Woods, The AI-Driven Leader, companion resources at aileadership.com

    You are here: the guardrails. Responsible AI / 07

    Using AI responsibly.

    In this section, you will learn six habits that keep you and Vanderbilt on solid ground, then test your judgment on four scenarios. By the end, you will know the never-paste list.

    You’ll learnSix guardrail habits and the never-paste list You’ll doWork through four workplace scenarios Time7 min
    The idea

    The model predicts rather than checks, so you verify, disclose, and keep a human on every decision. What you paste can leave Vanderbilt’s control, so anything sensitive goes to Amplify, or nowhere.

    Verify outputs

    AI writes confidently even when it's wrong. Check names, numbers, dates, and citations before anything leaves your desk.

    Protect data

    FERPA and HIPAA don't pause for convenience. Personnel records, health info, and anything confidential go to Amplify only, or nowhere.

    Disclose AI use

    If AI helped draft something substantive, say so. Simple transparency ("drafted with AI assistance, reviewed by me") builds trust.

    Human makes the call

    AI advises; you decide. Hiring, promotion, funding, discipline: decisions about people always need a person accountable for them.

    Bias awareness

    Models learn from human writing, biases included. Watch for skewed assumptions, especially in anything touching hiring, promotion, or evaluation.

    Keep learning

    These tools change monthly. A little steady curiosity keeps your skills compounding while everyone else is still skeptical.

    Never paste these into a public AI tool

    • Social Security numbers
    • Student records or grades (FERPA)
    • Health information (HIPAA)
    • Salaries & personnel files
    • Donor records & gift data
    • Passwords, keys & credentials
    • Unpublished research data
    • Contracts & legal matters

    Full guidance: Vanderbilt’s AI guidance and data classification policy will be linked here when published; until then, both are available through Human Resources and ITS.

    Practice

    Four judgment scenarios

    Reasonable people disagree on these four scenarios; that is part of the point.

    The idea

    AI moves faster than any handbook, so the gray areas land on regular staff first. Practicing on low-stakes scenarios beats improvising on real ones. The safe-looking middle option is not automatically the right one.

    Scenario 01

    The confident citation

    A colleague asks you to look over a report before it goes to their director. It reads beautifully (clearly AI-assisted), but one citation is to a study you’re fairly sure doesn’t exist.

    Our take: the report can’t go out with a fabricated source; that part isn’t negotiable. But start with the colleague: this is almost always ignorance, not fraud. Escalate only if it doesn’t get fixed.

    Principle: Accountability & integrity
    Scenario 02

    The medical-leave email

    You’re drowning in email. A long message arrives from an employee on your team describing the health situation behind their upcoming medical leave. You’re tempted to paste it into a public AI tool for a quick summary.

    Our take: this is protected health and personnel territory, HIPAA-adjacent at minimum. If AI touches it at all, only in Amplify. And for a short, personal, high-stakes message, your own attention might be the whole point.

    Principle: Human dignity & data protection
    Scenario 03

    The name test

    Your unit pilots an AI tool that pre-screens job applications. You run a quiet test: two nearly identical resumes, different names, and they come back with noticeably different scores.

    Our take: models inherit bias from the data they learned on. Document it, report it, and insist a human owns every hiring decision. This is exactly where “the AI ranked them” fails everyone.

    Principle: Bias & fairness
    Scenario 04

    The grant question

    AI helped you substantially draft a grant proposal: structure, phrasing, a first pass at the narrative. The ideas and data are yours. The sponsor’s forms don’t ask about AI use. Say anything?

    Our take: where the rules are unsettled, default to the disclosure you’d be comfortable defending in two years. University trust is slow to earn and quick to lose, and norms are moving toward disclosure, not away from it.

    Principle: Transparency & integrity

    Made-up but confident. The model predicts what sounds right, not what is right, so wrong answers arrive sounding great.

    3/8You crank the temperature setting way up. The model's output gets…

    More random. High temperature gives unlikely words a bigger chance: great for brainstorming, bad for budget memos.

    4/8Which prompt will get the best result?

    Context + role + interview + task. The CRIT shape from the builder and the Prompt Lab. Specific in, useful out.

    Amplify. Salary and personnel records are confidential; they only belong in Vanderbilt's approved platform.

    Sensitive data check. One second of "should this leave Vanderbilt?" prevents the mistakes that actually matter.

    8/8An AI gives you a beautifully written answer with two citations. What now?

    Verify it yourself. Models fabricate plausible-looking citations. Asking "are you sure?" just gets you a confident yes.

    What you now know.

    In this section, you will revisit the seven ideas you picked up today.

    How the machine writes

    An LLM predicts the likeliest next word, over and over. There is no lookup step and no database behind the answer.

    At work: draft with it freely, and never treat it as a source of record.

    Where it breaks

    Its abilities form a jagged frontier: it aces tasks you would bet against, flubs tasks you would bet on, and sounds equally confident on both.

    At work: verify names, numbers, dates, and citations before anything ships.

    Tasks, not jobs

    AI meets a role task by task: delegate the routine with oversight, redesign the middle together, and invest in the human core. Exposure is a development signal, never a verdict on a person.

    At work: one task inventory turns AI anxiety into a development plan.

    How to steer it

    CRIT (Context, Role, Interview, Task) turns a vague request into a brief. The signature move: tell the model to ask clarifying questions first, and wait. You stay the thought leader.

    At work: prompts come back needing fewer rewrites, and the questions it asks up front are the gaps you would otherwise find while checking.

    Where the work belongs

    Tool choice is a data decision first: sensitive or institutional data goes to Amplify, Office work suits Copilot, and public material is your choice.

    At work: one second of routing prevents the mistakes that are hardest to undo.

    Staying in charge

    You own every output, you disclose real AI help, and you decide where the saved time goes instead of letting new tasks swallow it.

    At work: judgment and boundaries are what make the new speed sustainable.

    What comes next

    Agents take goals instead of questions and work in steps under your oversight, on the same engine and with the same limits.

    At work: the skill they reward is the one you practiced here, clear and complete delegation.

    Go deeper: two ways people pair with AI

    The centaur divides the work with clean handoffs: you keep the strategy, the judgment calls, and anything with a name on it; AI gets the drafting, summarizing, and reformatting, with a clear line between the two. The cyborg weaves it together: drafts bouncing between you and the machine, every paragraph a little of both. Faster and more fluid, but it demands sharper verification because the seams disappear.

    Both styles come from Ethan Mollick’s essay on the 758-consultant experiment from the Limits module, and both beat working alone. Our suggestion: start centaur, because clean handoffs are easier to check, then grow cyborg as you learn the frontier. This platform’s stance is borrowed from his book Co-Intelligence: treat AI like a tireless, overconfident colleague, and always check its work.

    Source: Ethan Mollick, “Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier” (One Useful Thing, 2023) · Co-Intelligence: Living and Working with AI (2024).
