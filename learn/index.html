Module A

What is AI, really?

Five terms you'll hear constantly. Flip each card for the plain definition and a Vanderbilt example. (Click or press Enter.)

You’ll learnWhat AI, ML, GenAI, LLMs, and agents really are You’ll doFlip five cards Time2 min

Why it matters to your work: you’ll stop nodding along in meetings and start knowing which AI conversations actually concern your job.

The idea

These four terms nest inside each other like dolls. AI is the umbrella: any software doing things that used to need human judgment. Machine learning is how modern AI gets built: instead of a programmer writing rules, the system finds patterns in thousands of examples. Generative AI is machine learning that produces new things (text, images, code) instead of just sorting or scoring. And a large language model is the specific generative engine inside ChatGPT, Amplify, and Copilot. The newest doll is agentic AI: an LLM handed tools and a loop so it can carry out multi-step work on its own. It runs on the same engine as the chatbots, so it inherits the same limits.

Notice as you flip: the common thread is that none of these systems understand anything. They recognize patterns, and that one fact will follow us through the whole platform.

Go deeper: how learned systems differ from written rules

Rules vs. learned. Traditional software follows instructions a human wrote: if the form is missing a signature, reject it. A learned system studied thousands of examples and wrote its own internal rules, millions of them, none readable by us. The trade-off is real flexibility in exchange for imperfect predictability.

Why generative models err. They don’t retrieve facts from a filing cabinet; they reconstruct patterns. That’s why the same engine can draft a lovely paragraph and confidently misremember a date: both are pattern completions, and only one happens to be true.

Already on campus. ML has quietly run parts of university life for years: spam filters, fraud alerts on procurement cards, building-energy optimization, journal recommendations in the library. Generative AI is not the first wave of AI on campus, just the first one you can talk to.

Module B

How an LLM actually works

An LLM writes by predicting the next word, one word at a time. Here's a live (canned-data) peek at what that prediction looks like.

You’ll learnWhy AI writes so well and lies so confidently You’ll doPlay with a live prediction demo and its temperature dial Time3 min

Why it matters to your work: once you’ve seen the guesswork underneath, you’ll never skip verifying an AI answer again.

The idea

During training, the model read a huge slice of human writing and learned which words tend to follow which. That was the entire education. It writes by scoring every word it knows, picking a high scorer, adding it to the sentence, then doing it again, one word at a time, until the answer sounds finished. The temperature dial below controls how adventurous each pick gets to be.

Notice as you play: the model never checks anything. There is no lookup step, only pattern completion. That single fact explains almost everything else on this page.

“The staff meeting has been moved to ?”

1.0

Prediction: the model scores every word it knows and usually picks a high scorer. No plan, no opinion, just very good statistics.

Temperature: slide it up and the model gets adventurous, giving unlikely words a real chance. Great for brainstorming, risky for facts.

Hallucinations: because it predicts what sounds right rather than checks what is right, it can state made-up things with total confidence. Always verify.

The research

This is why the twenty minutes you’re spending here pays back. In a randomized experiment published in Science, Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang gave 453 professionals occupation-specific writing tasks. The group using ChatGPT finished 40% faster and produced work rated 18% higher in quality, and the tool narrowed the gap between weaker and stronger writers instead of widening it. The prediction machine you just played with is the same one behind those numbers.

Noy & Zhang, “Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence,” Science, 2023

Go deeper: temperature, memory, and what “training” really was

Temperature is a dial on the dice. Low, and the model almost always picks the top word: predictable, safer for facts. High, and the underdogs get real chances: fresher ideas, shakier accuracy. Neither is “correct”; they’re settings for different jobs.

A bigger window still isn’t memory. The model doesn’t remember your chat. The whole conversation is re-read on every turn, inside a fixed window. When the window fills, the earliest turns fall out the back. That’s the drift you feel in marathon chats, and why a fresh start with a clean summary often beats soldiering on.

“Training” wasn’t reading. It was billions of rounds of guess-the-next-word on public text, nudging internal dials after every miss until the guesses got eerily good. Everything you’ve watched it do flows from that one game.

Drill

Spot the hallucination

Four AI-style answers about plausible campus topics. One detail in each is invented. Click the phrase you don’t trust. (Every answer here was written for this drill; treat the “facts” accordingly. That’s rather the point.)

The idea

A hallucination is the machine doing what it was built to do: producing the most plausible-sounding next words. When the truth is common in its training data, plausible and true line up. But ask about something specific, rare, or internal (a policy number, a citation, a program name) and the model still completes the pattern, and the result sounds exactly as confident as a real fact.

In this drill you’ll practice spotting the warning signs of a made-up answer: a suspiciously precise number, a citation that looks real but isn’t, or a program name that sounds official. (Card players call these “tells.”) The danger: a wrong AI answer reads just as smoothly and confidently as a right one. You cannot tell the difference by tone, only by checking.

Next: the four built-in limits those myths grow out of. Once you know them, you’ll know when to trust an answer and when to check it.

Module D

Limits to know

Four built-in quirks of every LLM. None of them are dealbreakers once you know the counter-move.

The idea

Every limit on these cards is a practical consequence of what you just watched in Module B. Limited memory (the context window), staleness (the training cutoff), randomness (two runs, two answers), and unearned confidence (no self-doubt meter) all follow from one design decision: the model predicts text; it doesn’t consult anything.

Notice as you flip: each limit comes paired with a counter-move. A limit you can name is a limit you can work around.

The evidence: the jagged frontier

The largest workplace experiment to date put numbers on both sides of these limits. In 2023, Fabrizio Dell’Acqua, Ethan Mollick, and colleagues at Harvard Business School and BCG gave 758 consultants real consulting tasks. With AI, they completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, at 40% higher quality, all on work inside AI’s abilities. But on a task deliberately chosen to sit just beyond those abilities, consultants using AI fell from 84% correct to 60–70%. Their answers still sounded polished and confident; they were just wrong more often. The quality of the writing hid the drop in accuracy.

The researchers’ name for this is the jagged frontier: AI’s abilities form an edge that is spiky, not smooth. It aces some tasks you’d bet against and flubs some you’d bet on, so the working skill is knowing which side of the edge you’re standing on. And since the edge is invisible from the outside, the only reliable test is the habit this module keeps landing on: verify.

Dell’Acqua et al., “Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier,” Harvard Business School working paper, 2023 (PDF)

Module E

Five techniques that change everything

Small habits, outsized results. Copy any example and adapt it, then go practice them for real in the Playground below.

The idea

Why does prompting matter at all? Because the model cannot read your mind; it completes your pattern. A vague prompt hands it a vague pattern, and it will happily complete that too, with confident generic filler. Each technique below is a way of narrowing the pattern (with examples, grounding text, or a fresh start) until the likeliest continuation is the one you wanted.

1

Show examples (few-shot)

The fastest way to get your style is to show it, not describe it.

Here are two subject lines I like: “Quick favor, big impact” and “Three things before Friday.” Write five more in the same voice.
2

Iterate: the first answer is a first draft

Treat it like one. The second ask is usually the one that lands.

Good start. Now make it half as long, twice as warm, and end with one clear next step.
3

Give it your document

Grounding the model in your text beats its memory every time.

Using ONLY the policy text below, answer: can a new hire carry over vacation days? Quote the relevant line. [paste policy]
4

Ask it to critique you, or itself

It’s a surprisingly honest editor, especially of its own work.

Before you finalize, list three ways this draft could fail with a busy reader. Then revise it to fix all three.
5

Start fresh when it drifts

Long chats accumulate confusion. A clean start beats wrestling.

New chat: You are an experienced editor. Context you need: [two-sentence summary of where we left off]. Task: continue from there.
The research, made at Vanderbilt

The techniques you’re about to practice were catalogued on this campus. In 2023, Jules White of Vanderbilt Computer Science and his colleagues published “A Prompt Pattern Catalog to Enhance Prompt Engineering with ChatGPT”, the paper that turned prompting folk wisdom into named, reusable patterns. The Prompt Lab’s Role field below is White’s Persona pattern, and his free Coursera course, Prompt Engineering for ChatGPT, has become one of the most-taken AI courses anywhere.

You are here: judgment. Now that you can use the tools, let’s talk about using them well. Ethics / 05

Where policy ends, judgment begins.

Ethics is what you reach for when the handbook hasn't caught up yet. No compliance checkboxes here, just five lenses for thinking clearly and four dilemmas to practice on.

You’ll learnFive lenses for AI judgment calls You’ll doWork through four workplace dilemmas Time4 min

Why it matters to your work: sooner or later one of these lands on your desk, and it’s far easier to think it through now than at 4:55 p.m. on a Friday.

Bias & fairness

Models learn from oceans of human writing and decisions, and they inherit human bias along the way. In hiring or promotion support, “the algorithm said so” can quietly repeat old unfairness at new speed.

Transparency & disclosure

People deserve to know when AI meaningfully shaped something they’re reading, judging, or relying on. Disclosure is a form of respect, not a confession.

Accountability

You own what you send. “The AI did it” has never once worked as a defense, and it never will. If your name is on it, your judgment is in it.

Academic & professional integrity

Universities run on attribution, originality, and trust. Passing off AI work as purely your own spends credibility the whole institution depends on.

Human dignity & labor

These tools were trained on human work, and automation changes colleagues’ days. Involve people in changes that affect them; don’t let efficiency decisions simply happen to them.

Practice

What would you do?

Pick your honest instinct, then compare notes. Reasonable people disagree on these. The goal is knowing why you'd choose, not passing a test.

The idea

Rules answer the questions someone already thought of; judgment handles the ones nobody has yet. AI is moving faster than any handbook can, so the gray areas land on regular staff first, usually on a busy afternoon. Practicing on low-stakes scenarios now beats improvising on a real one later.

Notice as you choose: the “safe-looking” middle option isn’t automatically the right one. Each scenario turns on which principle is at stake.

Scenario 01

The confident citation

A colleague asks you to look over a report before it goes to their director. It reads beautifully (clearly AI-assisted), but one citation is to a study you’re fairly sure doesn’t exist.

Our take: the report can’t go out with a fabricated source; that part isn’t negotiable. But start with the colleague: this is almost always ignorance, not fraud. Escalate only if it doesn’t get fixed.

Principle: Accountability & integrity
Scenario 02

The medical-leave email

You’re drowning in email. A long message arrives from an employee on your team describing the health situation behind their upcoming medical leave. You’re tempted to paste it into a public AI tool for a quick summary.

Our take: this is protected health and personnel territory, HIPAA-adjacent at minimum. If AI touches it at all, only in Amplify. And for a short, personal, high-stakes message, your own attention might be the whole point.

Principle: Human dignity & data protection
Scenario 03

The name test

Your unit pilots an AI tool that pre-screens job applications. You run a quiet test: two nearly identical resumes, different names, and they come back with noticeably different scores.

Our take: models inherit bias from the data they learned on. Document it, report it, and insist a human owns every hiring decision. This is exactly where “the AI ranked them” fails everyone.

Principle: Bias & fairness
Scenario 04

The grant question

AI helped you substantially draft a grant proposal: structure, phrasing, a first pass at the narrative. The ideas and data are yours. The sponsor’s forms don’t ask about AI use. Say anything?

Our take: where the rules are unsettled, default to the disclosure you’d be comfortable defending in two years. University trust is slow to earn and quick to lose, and norms are moving toward disclosure, not away from it.

Principle: Transparency & integrity

Use it boldly. Use it well.

Six habits that keep you (and Vanderbilt) on solid ground. They take seconds and save careers. (For the deeper judgment calls behind these habits, see Ethics, just above.)

You’ll learnSix guardrail habits and the never-paste list You’ll doRead it once, remember it forever Time2 min

Why it matters to your work: these habits take seconds and protect colleagues, the institution, and your own good name.

The idea

These six habits follow from two facts you already know. Because the model predicts rather than looks anything up (Module B), you verify, you disclose, and you keep a human on every decision. And because what you paste can leave Vanderbilt’s control, the data rules route anything sensitive to Amplify, or nowhere. Hold onto those two facts and every card below becomes obvious.

Verify outputs

AI writes confidently even when it's wrong. Check names, numbers, dates, and citations before anything leaves your desk.

Protect data

FERPA and HIPAA don't pause for convenience. Personnel records, health info, and anything confidential go to Amplify only, or nowhere.

Disclose AI use

If AI helped draft something substantive, say so. Simple transparency ("drafted with AI assistance, reviewed by me") builds trust.

Human makes the call

AI advises; you decide. Hiring, promotion, funding, discipline: decisions about people always need a person accountable for them.

Bias awareness

Models learn from human writing, biases included. Watch for skewed assumptions, especially in anything touching hiring, promotion, or evaluation.

Keep learning

These tools change monthly. Fifteen curious minutes a week keeps your skills compounding while everyone else is still skeptical.

AI saves time, if you let it.

The promise was that AI reduces work. The research says: only if you're deliberate. A February 2026 Harvard Business Review study, “AI Doesn’t Reduce Work: It Intensifies It” by Ranganathan & Ye (they followed about 200 employees at a U.S. tech company for eight months), found AI often intensifies work instead. Three patterns to watch, and a counter-move for each.

You’ll learnHow AI quietly intensifies work, per the HBR research You’ll doSpot the three patterns, run a six-question self-check Time3 min

Why it matters to your work: the hour AI saves you only counts if you actually get to keep it.

Task expansion

When AI makes tasks feel lighter, people don’t do less; they take on more. In the study, workloads grew because “just one more thing” started feeling free.

On campus: you automate the weekly report Monday… and volunteer for two new committees by Thursday.

Counter-move: bank the savings. Decide in advance where saved time goes, and don’t reinvest all of it in new tasks.

Boundary blur

AI makes micro-work so easy it seeps into moments that used to be recovery: breaks, commutes, evenings. The workday stops having edges.

On campus: a “quick” prompt session at 9:40 p.m., because it “only takes a minute.”

Counter-move: AI hours ≠ more hours. If it happens in a break, it’s work. Protect recovery time like you’d protect a meeting.

Multitasking load

Running several AI threads at once feels productive, but the constant cognitive switching adds real fatigue, and the study links it to worse output.

On campus: three chat tabs, a draft memo, and a Copilot pane, and somehow nothing finished.

Counter-move: sequence, don’t parallelize. One AI-assisted task at a time: finished, then the next.

Working styles

Centaur or cyborg: both are right

In the same 758-consultant study from the Limits module, the top performers worked with AI in two distinct styles, and both beat working alone. Neither is the “advanced” one; they’re temperaments.

The centaur: divide the work

Like the mythical half-and-half: clean handoffs between human tasks and AI tasks. You keep the strategy, judgment calls, and anything with a name on it; AI gets the drafting, summarizing, and reformatting, with a clear line between the two.

On campus: you outline the announcement and decide the message; AI writes the first draft; you take back the final edit.

The cyborg: weave it together

Constant back-and-forth: half-finished sentences handed over, drafts bouncing between you and the machine, every paragraph a little bit of both. Faster and more fluid, but it demands sharper verification instincts because the seams disappear.

On campus: writing a report in a running conversation: “tighten this,” “what am I missing?”, “three versions of that closing line.”

The evidence

Both styles come from Ethan Mollick’s essay on the same HBS/BCG experiment, and both are legitimate. Our suggestion: start centaur, since clean handoffs are easier to verify while your instincts are young, and grow cyborg as your sense of the frontier gets calibrated. The guiding stance of this whole platform is borrowed (paraphrased, not quoted) from his book Co-Intelligence: treat AI like a person (a tireless, overconfident colleague), always check its work, and stay the human in the loop.

Shift 03

The human skill stack

Five human skills that grow more valuable as AI improves. The better AI gets at routine work, the more these skills stand out.

The idea

Why these five? Because they’re about to get scarce. As AI absorbs the drafting, summarizing, and assembling, the things machines can’t supply (choosing well, checking truth, briefing clearly, earning trust, knowing your unit’s exceptions) become the missing ingredient in every workflow. Better AI doesn’t threaten these skills; it makes them more valuable. The builder below turns your pick of them into a 90-day plan.

Judgment

AI produces options; someone still has to choose, and own the choice. The more drafts machines produce, the more valuable the person who can pick one and explain why.

Build it this quarter: before accepting any AI draft, note one thing you’d change and why.

Verification

As AI makes drafts fast and cheap, checking what’s true becomes the valuable part. The person who can quickly tell a right answer from a plausible-sounding one becomes the safety rail for the whole team.

Build it this quarter: verify every checkable fact in one AI draft per week, and time yourself getting faster.

Delegation to AI

Working with agents is management: clear goals, good context, sensible checkpoints. People who brief AI well will simply get more done than people who don’t.

Build it this quarter: hand one recurring task to AI weekly, briefed like you’d brief a new colleague.

Relationship & trust work

Colleagues, donors, campus partners: the conversations that move a university can’t be automated, and freed hours make you better at them. This is where the saved time compounds.

Build it this quarter: bank one AI-saved hour per week into a real, unhurried human conversation.

Domain depth

AI amplifies expertise; it doesn’t replace it. Knowing what your unit uniquely knows (the history, the exceptions, the why) is what makes your AI output better than anyone else’s.

Build it this quarter: spend 15 min/week using AI as a tutor on your field’s hard parts, verifying its answers as you go.

How the machine writes

An LLM predicts the likeliest next word, over and over. There is no lookup step and no database behind the answer.

At work: verify names, numbers, dates, and citations before anything ships.
